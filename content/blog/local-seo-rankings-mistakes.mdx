I've audited well over a hundred local business websites at this point, and I can count on one hand the ones that didn't have at least three things actively working against them in Google's eyes. Nobody tells these businesses their site is bleeding rankings — there's no error message, no warning email. Traffic just quietly declines while a competitor with a worse-looking website eats their lunch in the map pack.

Here's what I actually find when I dig in, and how you can check your own site for the same problems this afternoon.

## NAP Consistency Is Still a Foundation, Not a Formality

Name, Address, Phone — if these don't match exactly across your website, Google Business Profile, Yelp, Facebook, and every directory you're listed on, you're sending mixed signals about whether your business is even real and stable. I still see "Suite 200" on one listing and "Ste 200" on another, or an old phone number lingering on a directory from three moves ago.

This isn't just about generic spam scoring. Local ranking algorithms lean heavily on consistency as a trust signal because inconsistent data is what fake or defunct businesses look like. A quick way to check: search your business name plus your city in quotes and go through the first three pages of results. If you find a mismatch, fix it at the source (Google Business Profile and your site) first, then work outward.

## Google Business Profile Signals Get Ignored After Setup

Almost every client comes to me having "done" their Google Business Profile — meaning they filled it out once in 2022 and never touched it again. That's a problem because GBP is arguably a stronger local ranking lever than anything on your actual website.

What actually moves the needle:

- **Fresh photos monthly** — not stock images, real photos of the work, the team, the location
- **Posts** at least every couple of weeks (offers, updates, project photos)
- **Review velocity and response rate** — Google cares less about your total review count and more about whether new ones keep coming in and whether you respond to them
- **Q&A section** — seed it yourself with the questions customers actually ask
- **Category accuracy** — a lot of businesses pick one primary category and never review whether the secondary categories still match what they offer

If your GBP hasn't been touched in over 60 days, that's usually the first thing I fix on a new account, and it's often the fastest visible movement a client sees.

## Thin Pages Pretending to Be Depth

This is the one that surprises people most. A business will have a "Services" page listing eight services in two sentences each, plus eight separate "service pages" that are each barely 150 words of boilerplate with the city name swapped in. Google reads that as thin, templated content — the exact opposite of what you want when the whole point of a dedicated service page is to prove topical depth.

A real service page needs enough substance to actually answer a buyer's questions: what the service involves, how pricing generally works, what makes your approach different, common concerns addressed, and local context that isn't just a find-and-replace on the city name. I'd rather a client have four genuinely useful service pages than twelve thin ones — depth beats volume every time in 2026's algorithm.

## E-E-A-T Isn't Optional Anymore

Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trust — Google's guidelines spell this out explicitly now, and local search is where it shows up most bluntly. If your site has no author bios, no real photos of your team, no licensing or certification info, no genuine case studies, you're asking Google (and your visitors) to trust a business it can't actually verify exists as described.

Concrete fixes I use on almost every project:

- Real photos of the owner/technician/team, not stock people in hard hats
- A short "about the person doing the work" blurb on service pages, not just the company
- Visible licensing, insurance, or certification numbers where relevant to the trade
- Genuine before/after or project photos instead of generic stock imagery

## Page Speed and Core Web Vitals Still Quietly Cap Your Ceiling

Speed isn't the dominant ranking factor people once claimed it was, but it's a real ceiling. I regularly see local sites loading five to eight seconds on mobile because of unoptimized hero images, bloated page builder plugins, and render-blocking scripts from six different marketing tools nobody remembers installing.

Run your homepage and your top service page through PageSpeed Insights. If your Largest Contentful Paint is over 2.5 seconds or your Cumulative Layout Shift is bouncing around, that's actual measurable friction — both for the algorithm and for the visitor who bounces before your content even loads.

## Schema Markup and Internal Linking Nobody Set Up

Two smaller technical items that punch above their weight:

- **LocalBusiness schema** with your NAP, hours, and service area filled in correctly. Most site builders leave this blank or half-configured by default.
- **Internal linking between service and location pages.** If your plumbing page never links to your "emergency plumbing in [city]" page, you're leaving Google (and users) to guess at the relationship between your pages instead of showing it explicitly.

## A Quick Self-Audit Checklist

Before you pay anyone for a full audit, run through this yourself:

1. Search "[business name] [city]" — check for NAP mismatches on page one and two
2. Check your GBP — when was the last photo or post added?
3. Open your three most important service pages — do they read like they were written for a human with a real question, or padded to hit a word count?
4. Run your homepage through PageSpeed Insights (mobile) — is LCP under 2.5s?
5. View page source and search for "LocalBusiness" — is schema present and filled in?
6. Click through your site — does each service page link to at least one related page?

If two or more of these come back bad, that's very likely costing you rankings right now, not hypothetically.
